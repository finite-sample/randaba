\title{What Covariate Balancing at Assignment Buys, and What It Costs}
\author{Gaurav Sood}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Experimenters increasingly assign treatment with algorithms that make the
arms look alike on baseline covariates and then analyse the data with
regression, which recovers most of what balancing buys. We ask when balancing
at assignment still pays, what it costs in protection against unmeasured
covariates, and how to keep inference exact, for two-arm experiments analysed
by ordinary least squares (OLS). Exact balance beats complete randomization
with OLS by at least $(N-1)/(N-1-p)$, and exactly $(N-3)/(N-p-3)$ with
Gaussian covariates: under 5 percent with a handful of covariates, which
explains published null results, but 20 to 35 percent at 17 to 25 covariates
per 100 units. The cost lies in the distribution of assignments. A grouped
design that anticlusters units into $K$ groups and randomizes whole groups
prices it in closed form: its error over unmeasured covariates has only $K-1$
degrees of freedom, any local optimum of the anticlustering carries a
checkable bound on its imbalance, and small groups balanced on means make
omitted squares and interactions up to 76 percent worse than complete
randomization. In a comparison of nine designs, local search from random
starts on the covariates and their squares came within 2.5 percent of the
exact-balance optimum on average, kept a light tail, roughly tripled
protection against omitted nonlinear terms, gave the most power for a
nonlinear outcome once covariates were many, and supported an exact
randomization test. The rule: with few
covariates per unit, randomize and adjust; with many, balance the covariates
and their squares by local search from random starts, and test by redrawing
the design.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Random assignment balances covariates on average, not in the assignment an
experimenter actually draws. Two remedies compete. One balances at
assignment: blocking, matching, rerandomization \citep{morgan2012}, optimal
partitioning \citep{bertsimas2015,kallus2018} and, increasingly,
anticlustering \citep{papenberg2026}. The other adjusts at analysis, usually
by ordinary least squares (OLS) on the covariates \citep{lin2013}. Asymptotic
theory says that once the analysis uses an optimal regression, balancing
brings no first-order gain \citep{li2020}. Simulations agree:
\citet{papenberg2026} find that anticlustering, rerandomization and
minimization barely raise the power of an adjusted analysis.

That leaves three questions an experimenter needs answered before choosing a
design. When does balancing at assignment still pay over regression? What does
it cost in protection against covariates that were not measured? And how is
inference kept exact? This note answers them for two-arm experiments analysed
by the difference in means or by OLS.

The answers are short. Balance pays by a degrees-of-freedom factor: exact
balance beats complete randomization with OLS by at least $(N-1)/(N-1-p)$ and,
with Gaussian covariates, by exactly $(N-3)/(N-p-3)$. That is a few percent with
a handful of covariates, which explains the published null results, and 20 to
35 percent at 17 to 25 covariates per 100 units. Balance costs randomness, and
the cost is a property of the whole distribution of assignments. Grouped
randomization, which anticlusters units into $K$ groups and randomizes whole
groups, prices that cost in closed form. Its mean squared error over
unmeasured covariates has only $K-1$ degrees of freedom, and small groups
balanced on means make omitted squares and interactions worse than complete
randomization. A comparison of designs (Section~\ref{sec:compare}) then gives
the practical rule: with few covariates per unit, randomize and adjust; with
many, balance the covariates and their squares by local search from random
starts, and test by redrawing the design.

Much of the framing is established. \citet{kallus2018,kallus2020} casts design
as a game against an adversarial outcome, shows that a single optimized
partition can be $N-1$ times worse than complete randomization, and constrains
randomized designs so that Fisher's test stays usable.
\citet{krieger2019} and \citet{kapelner2021,kapelner2019} show that local
search from random starts balances well while staying nearly random, and judge
designs by a high quantile of their error over unmeasured covariates.
\citet{li2018} and \citet{branson2024} give the asymptotics and power of
rerandomization. This note adds an exact finite-sample account against OLS, a
closed-form family that prices the cost of balance, a certificate of balance
for any local optimum of anticlustering, an exact measure of what happens to
omitted nonlinear structure, and a comparison that puts these on one scale.

\section{Setup}

There are $N$ units, $N/2$ per arm. The assignment is $W\in\{-1,1\}^N$ with
$\mathbf 1'W=0$. Unit $i$ has potential outcomes $Y_i(1)$ and $Y_i(0)$; write
$\mu_i=\{Y_i(1)+Y_i(0)\}/2$ and $\tau$ for the average effect. A design is a
distribution over $W$, with $\Sigma=\E[WW']$. For the difference in means,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:identity}
\hat\tau-\tau=\tfrac{2}{N}W'\mu
\end{equation}
exactly, for any potential outcomes. If the design is unchanged by swapping
labels, $\E W=0$, $\hat\tau$ is unbiased, its variance is
$(4/N^2)\mu'\Sigma\mu$, and $\tr\Sigma=N$.

For OLS of $Y$ on $(1,T,X)$ with $T=(W+1)/2$ and $p$ covariates, let $P$
project onto the centred covariates and $Q=W'PW$, the part of the assignment
the covariates explain. If $Y=\alpha+\tau T+X\beta+z$ with a constant effect,
then by Frisch--Waugh--Lovell
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ols}
\hat\tau_{\rm OLS}-\tau=\frac{2\,W'(I-P)z}{N-Q},
\end{equation}
with $z$ also absorbing the intercept. Two kinds of risk appear below:
\emph{randomization risk}, over $W$ with outcomes fixed, and \emph{model risk},
also over a distribution for $z$.

\section{What balance buys}\label{sec:buys}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:buys}
If $\E[z\mid X,W]=0$, $\operatorname{Cov}(z\mid X,W)=\sigma^2 I$ and $N-Q>0$
on every assignment, then
$\operatorname{MSE}(\text{design},\text{OLS})=4\sigma^2\,\E_W[1/(N-Q)]$,
whatever $\beta$. The minimum, $4\sigma^2/N$, is reached when $Q=0$ on every
assignment.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:gain}
Under complete randomization $\E[Q\mid X]=pN/(N-1)$, so the gain of an exactly
balanced design over complete randomization with OLS is at least
$(N-1)/(N-1-p)$. If the rows of $X$ are independent $N(0,I_p)$,
$Q/N\sim\mathrm{Beta}\{p/2,(N-1-p)/2\}$ and the gain is exactly
$(N-3)/(N-p-3)$ for $N>p+3$.
\end{corollary}

The gain does not depend on how well the covariates predict the outcome: it is
what OLS pays for estimating $p$ coefficients. No bound in $N$ and $p$ alone
caps it from above; for particular covariates it is arbitrarily large
(Appendix). The conditional variance is the textbook OLS formula, and
\citet[eq.~6]{kapelner2019} give the design-averaged form to third order; the
corollary puts it in exact form. With one covariate, the setting
\citet{papenberg2026} simulate, the gain is $17/16$ at $N=20$ and under one
percent at $N=300$. It reaches 21 percent at $p/N=0.17$ and 34 percent at
$p/N=0.25$ with $N=120$, the regime where adjustment-based asymptotics are
also strained \citep{lei2021}.

\section{What balance costs}\label{sec:costs}

Balance moves variance off the measured directions and onto the rest:
$\tr\Sigma=N$ for every label-symmetric design. Three properties of $\Sigma$
and the design's support measure the cost.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Worst case.} $\mu'\Sigma\mu\le\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)\|\mu\|^2$, the
  accidental-bias bound of \citet{efron1971}. A single partition has
  $\lambda_{\max}=N$, $N-1$ times complete randomization \citep{kallus2018}.
\item \emph{Tail.} Over unmeasured covariates modelled as random, the spread of
  the MSE grows with $\|\Sigma\|_F^2$ \citep{kapelner2021}.
\item \emph{Resolution.} A randomization test can reject only if no
  assignment carries more than $\alpha/2$ probability \citep{kallus2020}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Grouped randomization}

To make these costs explicit, partition the units into $K$ groups of $m=N/K$
($K$ even) by anticlustering, so that groups are alike on the covariates, and
assign $K/2$ whole groups to treatment. Let $G$ be the membership matrix,
$s$ the group labels, $W=Gs$, $D$ the $K\times p$ matrix of group-mean
deviations and $A=X_c'X_c$. With $K$ equal to the number of arms this is the
design of \citet{bertsimas2015}; for $K=N$ it is complete randomization.
Cluster rerandomization \citep{lu2023} differs: it takes clusters as given
and restricts their assignment.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:gk}
Conditional on the partition:
\begin{enumerate}
\item every unit has probability $1/2$ of treatment;
\item $\Sigma=\frac{K}{K-1}G(I-\mathbf 1\mathbf 1'/K)G'$, whose $K-1$ nonzero
  eigenvalues equal $N/(K-1)$, so the worst case is $(N-1)/(K-1)$ times
  complete randomization and $\|\Sigma\|_F^2=N^2/(K-1)$;
\item the smallest two-sided randomization $p$-value is $2/\binom{K}{K/2}$;
\item $\E[Q]=\frac{Km^2}{K-1}\tr(A^{-1}D'D)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Holding the worst case to a factor $C$ of complete randomization therefore
needs $K\ge1+(N-1)/C$: the groups must be small.

\subsection{The tail has $K-1$ degrees of freedom}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:tail}
Let $z$ have independent $N(0,\sigma^2)$ entries. The OLS error over the
design given $z$ is $z'Mz$ with $M=4(I-P)\E[WW'/(N-Q)^2](I-P)$, so over $z$ it
is distributed as $\sigma^2\sum_i\lambda_i\chisq_1$ with $\lambda_i$ the
eigenvalues of $M$. Under grouped randomization these are the eigenvalues of
the $K\times K$ matrix $4H^{1/2}(mI-m^2B)H^{1/2}$, where $B=DA^{-1}D'$ and
$H=\E[ss'/(N-m^2s'Bs)^2]$. Under exact balance ($D=0$) the MSE is
$(4\sigma^2/N)\,\chisq_{K-1}/(K-1)$.
\end{proposition}

Relative to $4\sigma^2/N$, the $q$ quantile of the MSE is then about
\[
\Bigl(1+\tfrac{\E[Q]}{N}\Bigr)\frac{\chisq_{K-1}(q)}{K-1}
\quad\text{against}\quad
\frac{N-3}{N-p-3}\,\frac{\chisq_{N-p-1}(q)}{N-p-1}
\]
for complete randomization with OLS (Figure~\ref{fig:tail}). Both approximations agree with the exact
computation to within 1.3 percent at $N=120$ (Appendix). Grouped
randomization wins on the mean for every $K$, but at the 95th percentile only
with groups of two or three and many covariates: at $N=120$ it never wins with
$p=5$, and wins with $p=20$ only at $K\ge40$.

\subsection{A certificate of balance}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:cert}
At any local optimum of the exchange method on whitened covariates, for groups
$g,h$,
$\|d_g-d_h\|\le\rho_{gh}/m$, where $\rho_{gh}$ is the smallest
$\|x_j-x_i\|/\cos\theta$ over $i\in g$, $j\in h$ with $\cos\theta>0$, and
$\theta$ is the angle between $x_j-x_i$ and $d_h-d_g$. Hence
$\E[Q]\le\sum_{g<h}\rho_{gh}^2/\{(K-1)(N-1)\}$.
\end{proposition}

The bound uses only the data and the partition, so it can be reported with the
design. Heuristically, extending the two-group argument of
\citet{krieger2019} to $K$ groups gives
$\E[Q]\approx c_p\,m^{-(1+2/p)}N^{-2/p}$; in simulations with $N$ from 240 to
960 and $p$ from 1 to 10 the fitted exponents were within 0.05 and 0.12 of
these. Balance in small groups is fast with few covariates and slow with many.

\subsection{Omitted structure}

Proposition~\ref{prop:tail} treats what the analysis omits as noise. A real
omission is usually a function of the covariates, such as a square or an
interaction, and it lines up with the partition.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:omit}
For an omitted component $f$ (for OLS, residualized on $(1,X)$), its MSE under
grouped randomization relative to complete randomization is
$R_f=\frac{N-1}{N}\,m\,s_b^2/V_f$, where $s_b^2$ is the variance of $f$'s group
means and $V_f$ its variance.
\end{proposition}

A random partition gives $R_f\approx1$. Mean-only anticlustering into small
groups gave $R_f$ of 1.33 to 1.76 for squares and interactions at $N=120$:
making each small group's mean equal the sample mean pairs high units with
low ones, which spreads the group means of $x^2$. Balancing the squares and
products as well brought $R_f$ to 0.41 to 0.81. Like the certificate, $R_f$ can
be computed for any candidate omission before the experiment runs.

\section{A comparison of designs}\label{sec:compare}

We compare designs where balance can pay. With $N=120$ and $p\in\{5,20,30\}$,
we draw 20 covariate matrices from $N(0,I_p)$. For each matrix and design we
draw 4{,}000 assignments and analyse every one by OLS on $(1,T,X)$. The
designs are complete randomization; rerandomization at a 0.1 percent
acceptance rate \citep{morgan2012}; greedy pair-switching \citep{krieger2019},
which runs the two-group exchange from a random start to a local optimum, on
the covariates, on the covariates and their squares, and (at $p=5$) on the
covariates, squares and pairwise products; optimal non-bipartite matched
pairs; and grouped randomization with pairs and groups of four, the latter
also with squares. Table~\ref{tab:compare} reports four things.
\begin{itemize}
\item The mean and 95th percentile over $z\sim N(0,I)$ of the OLS error,
  relative to exact balance, from 2{,}000 draws of $z$ (standard errors about
  0.001).
\item The effective sample size, relative to complete randomization, for an
  omitted noise-free signal made of the squares and pairwise products of three
  covariates.
\item The power of the randomization test that redraws the design, at an
  effect of 1.96 standard deviations of the complete-randomization estimate,
  for a linear outcome and for one with that nonlinear signal (standard errors
  0.01 to 0.03).
\item The size of the same test, averaged over the two outcomes.
\end{itemize}

Three results stand out.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Local search from random starts reaches the optimum of
  Proposition~\ref{prop:buys}.} Greedy pair-switching on the covariates has
  mean error 0.999 to 1.010 of exact balance at every $p$, so its gain over
  complete randomization with OLS (4, 20 and 33 percent) is nearly the
  $(N-3)/(N-p-3)$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:gain} (4, 21 and 34 percent). Its
  95th percentile is the lowest of all designs: restarts give it many
  near-optimal partitions, so it keeps nearly the degrees of freedom of
  complete randomization. Rerandomization at 0.1 percent stops short of the
  optimum once $p$ is large.
\item \emph{Balancing the squares as well costs little and buys a lot.} On the
  covariates and their squares, greedy pair-switching gives up 0.2 to 2.5
  percent of mean efficiency and some tail, but its effective sample size for
  the omitted signal is 2.9 to 3.3. Its power for the nonlinear outcome is
  0.44, 0.50 and 0.52 at $p=5$, 20 and 30, against 0.35 to 0.36 for complete
  randomization; at $p=20$ and 30 no design has more. Adding the products at $p=5$ balances the omitted
  signal almost exactly, because the signal consists of those products; it
  shows what targeted balance can do, not what to expect for an unknown
  nonlinearity.
\item \emph{Coarse randomization is the wrong way to buy robustness.} Grouped
  designs gain their randomness from one fixed partition. Their tail pays for
  it (Proposition~\ref{prop:tail}) and their mean is no better; with means
  alone, small groups lose on the omitted signal
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:omit}). Matched pairs help with the omitted signal
  but lose efficiency as $p$ grows.
\end{enumerate}
Every randomization test held its size: rejection rates under the null were
0.04 to 0.06.

\section{Discussion}

The gain from balancing at assignment over OLS is a degrees-of-freedom term.
It is negligible with a few covariates and worth a fifth to a third of the
sample when there are 17 to 25 covariates per 100 units. The cost of balance
lies in the distribution of assignments, and a design that draws from one
coarse partition pays it in the tail. Local search from random starts avoids
that cost because good anticlustering partitions are plentiful and unlike each
other \citep{papenberg2024}. Balance on the covariates and their squares,
because small groups and greedy moves that equalize means can unbalance
spread, and test by redrawing the design.

The results have limits. The effect is constant, so the randomization test
addresses the sharp null; inference for an average effect under heterogeneity
needs a studentized statistic \citep{wu2021}, and its behaviour under these
designs is not studied here. The tail criterion models unmeasured covariates
as independent noise, and Proposition~\ref{prop:omit} covers only specific
omitted functions. The rate after Proposition~\ref{prop:cert} is a heuristic;
a proof must handle the dependence the algorithm induces among groups. The
comparison uses Gaussian covariates and one sample size. A comparison with the
Gram--Schmidt Walk \citep{harshaw2024} and with the inference-constrained
design of \citet{kallus2020}, and an application to real data, are left for
future work.

\section{Proofs and checks}

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:buys}.} By Frisch--Waugh--Lovell and
\eqref{eq:ols}, given $W$ the variance is $4\sigma^2W'MW/(N-Q)^2$ with
$M=I-\mathbf 1\mathbf 1'/N-P$, and $W'MW=N-Q$.

\emph{Corollary~\ref{cor:gain}.} $\E[Q\mid X]=\tr(P\Sigma_{\rm CRD})=pN/(N-1)$;
Jensen's inequality gives the bound. With Gaussian rows the centred column
space of $X$ is a uniformly oriented $p$-dimensional subspace of
$\mathbf 1^\perp$, so $Q/N$ is the squared length of the projection of a fixed
unit vector, $\mathrm{Beta}\{p/2,(N-1-p)/2\}$, and
$\E[N/(N-Q)]=(N-3)/(N-p-3)$. A Monte Carlo check agreed to within one standard
error. For no upper bound: with $N=8$, $w_0=(1,1,1,1,-1,-1,-1,-1)$,
$v=(1,-1,1,-1,1,-1,1,-1)$ and $x=w_0+0.01v$, an exactly balanced assignment
exists and enumeration gives a gain of 287.

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:gk}.} $\E[ss']=\frac{K}{K-1}(I-\mathbf
1\mathbf 1'/K)$ and $G'G=mI$. Item 3 counts the $\binom{K}{K/2}$ equally likely
assignments, two per partition. Item 4: $G'X_c=mD$, so $Q=m^2s'DA^{-1}D's$, and
the rows of $D$ sum to zero.

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:tail}.} Square \eqref{eq:ols} and average over
$W$. Under grouped randomization $W=Gs$, $G'(I-P)G=mI-m^2B$, and the nonzero
eigenvalues of $XY$ and $YX$ coincide. With $D=0$, $H=\E[ss']/N^2$. The
reduction was checked against the $N\times N$ matrix built by enumerating every
assignment, and the resulting quantile against a brute-force quantile over
40{,}000 draws of $z$ (within 0.5 percent).

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:cert}.} The exchange objective is
$\sum_g m\|d_g\|^2$, and swapping $i\in g$ with $j\in h$ changes it by
$2\delta'(d_g-d_h)+2\|\delta\|^2/m$ with $\delta=x_j-x_i$. At a local optimum
this is non-negative, so $\|\delta\|\,\|d_g-d_h\|\cos\theta\le\|\delta\|^2/m$.
A pair with $\cos\theta>0$ exists because the average of $x_j-x_i$ over pairs is
$d_h-d_g$. Then $\sum_g\|d_g\|^2=K^{-1}\sum_{g<h}\|d_g-d_h\|^2$, whitening
gives $A=(N-1)I$, and item 4 of Proposition~\ref{prop:gk} gives the bound. It
held in all 400 simulated partitions.

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:omit}.}
$f'\Sigma_Gf=\frac{K}{K-1}m^2\sum_k(\bar f_k-\bar f)^2$ and
$f'\Sigma_{\rm CRD}f=\frac{N}{N-1}\|f-\bar f\|^2$; it was also checked by
enumeration.

enumeration and every simulation are at
